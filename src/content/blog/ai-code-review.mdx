Every week a new AI tool promises to change how we review code. GitHub Copilot, Cursor, CodeRabbit, PR-Agent. The list keeps growing. I've spent the last four years shipping full-stack code at SharinPix — Rails backend, TypeScript frontend, Redis for the queue — and I've been integrating these tools into real production workflows long enough to have a clear picture of what they actually do.

## What AI Gets Genuinely Right

The tools are impressive in narrow, specific ways. Surface-level issues — unused imports, missing null checks, inconsistent naming, obvious security patterns — get flagged faster than any human reviewer. When I ran an AI review on a 400-line pull request recently, it caught a SQL injection risk I'd skimmed past after two manual passes.

<CodeBlock filename="assets_controller.rb" lang="ruby" code={`# Before: what I wrote
def find_user_assets(user_id)
  Asset.where("user_id = #{user_id}")  # SQL injection waiting to happen
end

# After: AI flagged it immediately
def find_user_assets(user_id)
  Asset.where(user_id: user_id)
end`} />

That's a legitimate, consequential catch. The speed argument holds up too. A thorough human review of a complex PR can take 30 to 60 minutes. An AI gives you first-pass feedback in under a minute. For teams moving fast, that turnaround matters.

## The Limitations Nobody Talks About

AI tools don't understand why your code exists. They see patterns, not intent. They'll suggest making something more idiomatic without knowing that the verbose version exists specifically to aid debugging in production. They'll flag a seemingly redundant check that's actually a critical guard against a known edge case you've been burned by before.

<CodeBlock filename="soft_delete_service.rb" lang="ruby" code={`# AI suggestion: "use destroy instead of archived_at"
# Human context: archived_at is our soft delete convention.
# destroy triggers three downstream jobs and breaks the audit log.
def soft_delete(record)
  record.update(archived_at: Time.current)
end`} />

The AI would suggest replacing this with `record.destroy`. It's cleaner by every generic metric. It would also break three background jobs that check `archived_at` to determine record visibility. This is the fundamental gap: AI reviews code in isolation. Human reviewers review it in context.

  The tool caught the syntax. It had no idea what the method was protecting against.
</PullQuote>

## Four Places It Consistently Fails

The failure modes I've seen most often aren't random. They follow a pattern.

The first is domain memory. Your soft delete convention, your background job triggers, your specific API edge cases — the AI has never seen any of it. Every PR is day one for the model.

The second is architectural judgment. It can tell you a method is too long. It cannot tell you whether a feature belongs in the service layer or the model, or whether the design is solving the right problem at all.

The third is the noise problem. Fifteen comments on a twenty-line PR creates friction. Junior developers especially lose the signal in the volume. Many of those comments are style preferences, not problems, and treating them as equivalent is dangerous.

The fourth is confidence without calibration. It will suggest a pattern that works in isolation and ignore a constraint introduced two sprints ago. It doesn't know what it doesn't know, and it won't tell you when it's guessing.

## My Actual Workflow

After enough months of experimenting, here is how I use AI review: as a first pass, not a last one. I run the diff through an AI tool before I request a human review. I work through the mechanical feedback myself, clean up the obvious issues, and by the time it reaches a colleague the noise is already gone. That frees up my human reviewers to focus on what actually requires judgment — architecture decisions, business logic correctness, performance implications and the unwritten rules of our specific codebase.

One thing that made the AI feedback significantly more useful was giving it context upfront rather than just pasting the diff:

<CodeBlock filename="review_prompt.txt" lang="text" code={`Context: Rails 7 app, soft deletes via archived_at column,
background jobs use Sidekiq. This PR adds bulk upload
via AWS S3 pre-signed URLs.

Review this for: security issues, performance, consistency
with our patterns.

[diff here]`} />

The less it has to guess, the more useful it becomes. Treat it like onboarding a contractor, not consulting an oracle.

<PullQuote cite="Mohun Shakeel Ahmad">
  AI raises the floor of what hits the review queue. It doesn't replace the ceiling of what a great engineer brings to it.
</PullQuote>

## The Verdict

Is AI ready to replace your senior dev in code review? No. The capability gap on context, architecture and institutional knowledge is real and it matters on any codebase with meaningful complexity.

The right framing isn't replacement. It's leverage. Use it as a tireless first reviewer that handles the mechanical work, so your senior engineers can focus on the decisions that genuinely require them. The tools will get better. They always do. But right now the org chart still has a slot for someone who's been around long enough to know why that weird check is there.
