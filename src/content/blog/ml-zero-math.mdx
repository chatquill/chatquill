Every time I try to explain machine learning at a family dinner in Mauritius, eyes glaze over the moment I mention "algorithms." So I've gotten good at explaining it differently. No equations. No jargon. Just the actual idea.

## The Simple Version

Machine learning is teaching a computer to recognize patterns — the same way you learned to recognize your grandmother's handwriting without anyone explaining the rules to you.

<PullQuote>
  You didn't learn to read by memorizing every possible sentence. You learned patterns. ML does the same thing, just with numbers.
</PullQuote>

When you were a child, you learned what a "dog" is by seeing hundreds of dogs. Big ones, small ones, fluffy ones. Nobody gave you a formal definition. You **inferred the pattern**. Machine learning systems do the exact same thing — they're shown thousands or millions of examples and they figure out the pattern themselves.

## Three Types, Simply Put

**Supervised learning** — You show the system examples with answers. "This email is spam. This one isn't." After enough examples, it can classify new emails on its own. Most practical ML you encounter is this.

**Unsupervised learning** — You show the system examples without answers. "Here are 10,000 customers — find groups." It finds structure you didn't know to look for. Useful for discovery.

**Reinforcement learning** — The system learns by trial and error, earning rewards for good decisions. How AlphaGo learned to beat the world's best Go players. How your phone's autocomplete learns your typing habits.

## Why It Matters Now

ML isn't new — the core ideas are from the 1950s and 60s. What changed is data and compute. We now have more data than any human could process, and cheap enough computing to crunch it. That's it. The big AI moment isn't a new idea. It's an old idea at unprecedented scale.

Next time someone talks about "AI" at work or in the news, this is likely what they mean: a system that learned patterns from data and is now applying those patterns to new situations. No magic. Just math at scale.
